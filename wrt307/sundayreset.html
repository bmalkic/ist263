The Sunday Reset: Plan Your Whole Week in 30 Minutes

Five quick steps on Sunday (about 30 minutes) and one 10-minute check-in on Friday. Together they turn a pile of deadlines, readings, and “I should really…” thoughts into a calm, realistic week.

Start at Step 1 →Watch the video first

Before you start, grab:

  • A notebook or notes app
  • Your syllabi (or course pages) for every class
  • A calendar, paper or digital
  • A timer and about 30 minutes

Project goals

We created this site to teach one practical habit clearly enough that a first-year student could follow it alone. Our goals are to explain each step in plain language, show what it looks like with our own photos, and let you jump to any step when you need a refresher.

Who this is for

Our target audience is Syracuse University students, especially first- and second-years juggling several syllabi for the first time, though the method works for any student.

Home › Steps › Step 1

    Step 1 of 6Sunday⏱ ~5 min

    Step 1: Brain dump everything

    What to do

    1. Set a timer for five minutes.
    2. Write down every task, deadline, errand, and worry in your head.
    3. Don’t sort or judge anything yet. Just get it out.

    Your brain stops juggling once everything is on paper.

    JJordan’s exampleIn five minutes Jordan writes 12 items, including: read Ch. 4 for psychology, econ problem set, draft history essay intro, email advisor about spring classes, buy printer ink, laundry, and RSVP to the club soccer social.

    Sunny’s tip: Include small things like “email advisor.” Small tasks cause the most hidden stress.

    ✅ You’re done when you have one list with everything on it and nothing is left floating around in your head.

    Home › Steps › Step 2

      Step 2 of 6Sunday⏱ ~5 min

      Step 2: Lock in fixed commitments

      What to do

      1. Open your calendar.
      2. Add everything that cannot move: classes, work shifts, club meetings, and appointments.
      3. Add every deadline from your syllabi for the next two weeks.
      JJordan’s example
      • Classes: Mon/Wed/Fri mornings, Tue/Thu afternoons
      • Library shifts: Tue and Thu, 6–9 p.m.
      • Club soccer: Mon and Wed, 8 p.m.
      • Deadlines: econ problem set due Thursday night, history essay draft due Friday

      Sunny’s tip: Color-code classes, work, and deadlines so you can read the week at a glance.

      ✅ You’re done when your calendar shows every class, shift, meeting, and deadline for the next two weeks.

      Home › Steps › Step 3

        Step 3 of 6Sunday⏱ ~5 min

        Step 3: Rank your tasks

        What to do

        1. Go back to your brain dump from Step 1.
        2. Mark each item:
          • 1 = due this week or high stakes
          • 2 = important but flexible
          • 3 = nice to do
        3. Write a rough time estimate next to each item.
        4. Add 25% to every estimate, because everything takes longer than you think.
        JJordan’s example
        • Econ problem set: 1 · 2 hrs → 2.5 hrs
        • History essay intro: 1 · 1.5 hrs → about 2 hrs
        • Email advisor: 2 · 10 min → 15 min
        • Buy printer ink: 3 · 20 min → 25 min

        Sunny’s tip: If everything feels like a 1, ask yourself, “What happens if this waits a week?” If the answer is “not much,” it’s a 2.

        ✅ You’re done when every item has a 1, 2, or 3 and a padded time estimate.

        Home › Steps › Step 4

          Step 4 of 6Sunday⏱ ~10 min

          Step 4: Time-block your week

          What to do

          1. Drag (or write) your priority 1 tasks into open calendar slots first.
          2. Then fit in your priority 2 tasks. Priority 3 tasks only go in if there’s room.
          3. Put your hardest work in the hours when you have the most energy. If you focus best in the morning, schedule your essay at 9 a.m., not 9 p.m.
          4. Leave at least two empty “overflow” blocks for anything that runs long.
          JJordan’s exampleJordan focuses best in the morning, so the essay intro goes Tuesday 9–11 a.m. The econ problem set goes Wednesday 1–3:30 p.m., a day before it’s due. Overflow blocks: Thursday 4:30–6 p.m. and Saturday 10 a.m.–noon.

          Sunny’s tip: Name each block with a specific action, such as “Draft intro paragraph,” not just “Study.”

          ✅ You’re done when every priority 1 task has a time slot and you have at least two overflow blocks.

          Home › Steps › Step 5

            Step 5 of 6Sunday⏱ ~5 min

            Step 5: Plan a “minimum day”

            What to do

            1. Write down the three tasks that must happen even on a bad day, and nothing else.
            2. Put the note somewhere you’ll see it.

            Some days fall apart. When life gets chaotic, you do your minimum day and still stay on track.

            JJordan’s exampleJordan’s minimum day: (1) go to class, (2) do 30 minutes of the econ problem set, (3) reply to the group project chat.

            Sunny’s tip: Even Sunny has slow days. Keep the note where a bad day can’t hide it: your laptop, your phone lock screen, or your mirror.

            ✅ You’re done when you have exactly three tasks written down somewhere you’ll see them. That wraps up your Sunday!

            Home › Steps › Step 6

              Step 6 of 6Friday⏱ ~10 min

              Step 6: Do a Friday check-in

              This step happens later in the week, not on Sunday. On Friday afternoon, take ten minutes to look back.

              What to do

              1. Ask: What got done? Check it off.
              2. Ask: What kept slipping? Move unfinished tasks to next week’s list.
              3. Compare your time estimates to how long things actually took, and adjust for next week.

              Over a few weeks, your plans become much more accurate.

              JJordan’s exampleThe econ problem set is done. The essay intro took 3 hours instead of 2, so Jordan will estimate essay work higher next week. The advisor email slipped again, so it becomes the first task on Monday morning.

              ✅ You’re done when unfinished tasks are on next week’s list and you’ve noted at least one estimate to adjust.
